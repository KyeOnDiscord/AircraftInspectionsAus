Aircraft Ownership - Some Of The Perils

The treacherous maze of buying an aircraft has seen many wise men kneel down and cry.

So, let’s explore some of the traps and what one can do to mitigate risks once you make a decision to step into the world of aircraft ownership

Firstly, one must identify with certainty the purpose your aircraft will serve. Do you need an aircraft for private travel or do you intend to offer flights for hire or reward or perhaps your intention is to provide aerial surveillance services. This will determine if your aircraft is required to be certified in Private, Air Transport or Aerial Work operational categories. Do you intend for your aircraft to be flown only in visual conditions or in all weather conditions, requiring instrument flight rules certification? The equipment requirements are vastly different between different operational categories.

Perhaps you intend for your aircraft to serve more than one function, as is frequently the case. Consider carefully the right aircraft for you and the proposed operations your aircraft will be engaged in. For example, you may source an attractive low wing machine, but by committing to such purchase you have immediately limited functions your aircraft can perform. Scenic Flight operations and Aerial Photography cannot be performed efficiently in low wing aircraft as much of the ground visibility is obscured by wings.

Your intention may be to commence a skydiving enterprise, this is admirable, but unless you purchase an aircraft which has: ‘Operations With Door Removed Supplement’ your aspirations will not come to fruition.

The list of blunders one can make when purchasing an aircraft is almost endless. A classic blunder which comes to mind is one of an American entrepreneur who made his first aircraft purchase recently. This was seemingly a superb buy, a turbine powered multi-engine multi crew aircraft which had undergone and cruised through all the engineering checks commissioned by the buyer. Seemingly this machine was advertised and sold for well below market value. Following a short period of jubilation, the buyer realised that he missed the clause in the Sales Contract which stated that Pratt and Whitney owned the engines which powered his aircraft. Power by the Hour engine programs are becoming increasingly popular. This is where another entity owns your aircraft engines and the owner is liable to make payments to this entity based on hourly utilisation of engines. The moral of this example is to have a clear understanding of what you are actually buying and any encumbrances.

Aircraft ownership can be a rewarding and commercially viable experience but obtain professional guidance to ensure you make the right decisions.
